The width charge: what the clean backtest says

Executive summary of muni_width_charge_research, run 2026-11-02 on the desk's requests from June to October 2026 (1.28 million winnable bid requests). Prepared for the desk and QR.

At a realistic size the width charge is safe and roughly neutral. It is not the 120 thousand dollars a month we reported last week. That figure came from a charge that was five times larger than intended and from a different exit valuation.

With the traders' manual charge left in place and the model's charge set to 2 bp on a typical bond, the desk keeps 89% of its wins at the same win rate, leaves 0.15 bp less on the table against the cover, and earns between 0 and 0.03 bp more per request depending on how the exit is valued, about 45 thousand dollars a month on the exit of record, with a t of 2 to 3. Under the strictest valuation it is exactly flat. Our recommendation is to keep it as a candidate, not to put it live as a P&L engine.

What the model does, in plain terms

Production bids every bond of a key with the same charge. The width charge adds a second, bond-specific charge: how far the bond's own price is expected to wander over the week after we buy it, measured from the factor model's residual and calibrated on our own past fills. A bond whose move is predictable gets a small extra charge, a dispersed one gets a large one. The charge can only widen our bid, so it can only lose us wins, never win something production would have let go. It applies on the 26% of requests where the bond is in the factor model's coverage; the rest quote exactly as production.

Everything in the backtest is paired on identical requests. The win on each request is decided by the logged cover or the winning print, so only the quote moves. The replay reproduces production's own quote and every logged win and loss before anything is scored, and the replay's wins match the desk's WFTrade fills 99.5% of the time with the same par on 95% of CUSIP-days, so the inventory the backtest builds is the desk's.

Where the money comes from, and why that matters

The charge touches only the 26% of requests with a band. On those, giving up the widest-band wins loses money under every valuation; the loss grows with the width. The positive total comes from the other 74%, where the model quotes exactly as production while the comparator, production widened uniformly to the same win count, gives up wins it should have kept. In other words, selective widening beats uniform widening at the same win rate. That is true of almost any targeted charge and is not evidence that this measure picks out bad wins.

The right-hand panel is the finding to take away. On this run's valuations, the bonds with the widest bands are the ones the desk won by the most (15 bp against the cover versus 3 bp on tight bonds), marked out best, and resold at the widest markups. A charge that targets them trades away the desk's most profitable fills. The premise that dispersed bonds are adverse selection does not hold here.

What changed since last week's number

  • The charge was five times too large. The scale was set on the first month of data, where the band had not yet been calibrated and read 2.4 bp at the median. Applied to calibrated bands of 10 to 16 bp from June on, the "2 bp" charge was 8 to 13 bp. The 120 thousand a month, the 30% win churn and the over-widening of the extended version all came from that. Fixed in this notebook: the scale is set on the first calibrated month, and 2 bp means 2 bp.
  • A different exit valuation. Last week's P&L used the lab's peer-bond exit; this notebook uses the comparable-bond bucket exit, which exists on 42% of requests and tracks realised sales with a correlation of 0.21, beside the bond's own next print, which tracks them at 0.72 and is the strictest reading here. The two runs are not on the same footing, and the strict exit says flat.
  • The first clean run was invalid and was discarded. The pricing service was down, which removed every cover yield; the notebook now refuses to score in that state.

The variations

Fourteen settings were run: four sizes of the charge, two band models, the total move in place of the residual, and the charge extended to every request through a width model. All of them sit within 0.02 bp per request of zero with the manual charge in; four pass the sign test on cover, all three valuations and dollars, none by a margin that survives a change of exit. Smaller charges do not help (0.5 and 1 bp are slightly negative) and larger ones hurt (3 bp). Replacing the manual charge with this one fails outright: cover up 7 bp, half the wins kept.

Recommendation

  • Do not put it live as a P&L model. The only robust effects at a realistic size are 0.15 bp less cover and 11% fewer wins. The P&L is flat on the strictest valuation.
  • Keep the machinery. The replay is exact, the inventory reconciles to WFTrade, and the backtest now has a calibrated scale, a pricing guard and a decomposition that shows where any result comes from. Any future charge can be tested in an hour.
  • Two things worth testing next. Measure the band in dollars rather than yield (width times DV01), since the current measure falls hardest on short bonds, which carry the least dollar risk; and rerun with the lab's peer-bond exit to settle whether the gap to last week's number is the scale or the exit.

Definitions. Cover change: bp of yield left on the table against the best competing bid on the wins with a cover, model minus comparator; negative is better. P&L per request: the model's P&L minus the comparator's, averaged over all winnable requests, where a win is valued at its price plus the move to the exit. Dollars: the same, weighted by each request's DV01 and trimmed at the 99th percentile. The t beside each delta is week-clustered. Nothing here is a threshold; every cell is a change against the comparator.
